Meter moving, bill up, warm floor: what a hidden leak looks like

Any one of these signs is reason enough to test. Leak detection answers all of them, because it finds the exact spot before anyone cuts into drywall, tile or the yard.

Meter keeps moving

With every faucet, appliance and ice maker off, a turning dial or spinning leak indicator means water is leaving the system.

Bill jumped

A higher water and sewer bill with no change in habits often traces to a slow leak, a running toilet or a sprinkler line.

Warm spot on the floor

A warm patch on a slab or tile can mean a hot water line is leaking in or under the concrete.

Stain on the ceiling

A brown ring or bubbling paint shows where water came to rest, which is often not where it started.

Hissing in a wall

A faint hiss or trickle with nothing running usually means a pressurized line is leaking inside the wall.

Soggy yard or weak pressure

A wet strip of lawn in dry weather, or pressure that sags through the whole house, can mean a break on the line from the meter.

Active leak right now: what to do first

Water that is already spraying or pooling needs these steps first.

  • Close the main shutoff valve, usually where the line enters the house or near the water heater.
  • Open the lowest faucet in the house to drain the lines and relieve pressure.
  • Stay out from under a dripping ceiling and keep away from wet outlets, light fixtures and the breaker panel.
  • Switch off the power or gas control to a leaking water heater so it does not run dry.
  • Photograph the water, the damage and the meter reading for your insurance claim, then move valuables out of the wet area.

Reading your meter and running a leak test

A meter test takes no tools and tells the technician whether to start inside the house or out in the yard.

  1. Find the meter

    Look for a lidded box near the curb or property line, or a meter inside a utility area. Lift the lid carefully and watch for insects.

  2. Stop all water use

    Close every tap, pause the irrigation controller and make sure no washer, dishwasher or ice maker is running.

  3. Read the dial

    Note the digits and the unit printed on the face. Many meters also have a small leak indicator, often a triangle or star, that turns when any water moves.

  4. Wait and read again

    Leave everything off for at least 30 minutes, longer if you can, then read again. A changed number or a turning indicator confirms a leak.

  5. Close the house main and repeat

    If the meter stops with the main closed, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the buried line between the meter and the house.

Two utilities serve Scott County: know who reads your meter

Georgetown Municipal Water & Sewer Service (GMWSS) is the city utility, while Kentucky American Water covers parts of northern Scott County, including Moon Lake, Deer Run and Mallard Point. Your bill names the utility that reads your meter, and that is the office to call about readings.

Ask that utility whether it can show daily or hourly usage. Steady use in the small hours, when nobody is up, is strong evidence of a leak.

Stains on the ceiling and leaks in walls or the attic

A ceiling stain tells you where water came to rest, not where it started. Water follows framing, wiring and pipes before it drips, so the source can be a bathroom above, a supply line in the wall, an air handler drain line or the roof.

The technician starts by checking whether the stain grows when upstairs fixtures run, then may use a moisture meter and thermal camera to map the wet material behind the surface. A pressure test on the supply lines separates a pressurized leak from a drain or roof leak. Hissing inside a wall usually means a supply line, while a stain that appears only after rain points upward to the roof.

Roof leaks and flashing failures belong to a roofer, and we will say so when testing points there. A leak on a supply or drain line usually needs only a small access opening that a drywall finisher can patch. Repeat leaks in the same old line are a reason to ask about replacing the line.

Leaks under the house and in the crawlspace

The dampness is the catch in Georgetown. On Bluegrass limestone, groundwater seepage alone can leave a crawlspace wet, so a damp floor does not prove a pipe is leaking. Run the meter test: if the meter moves while the space is wet, a pipe is involved. If it holds still, the moisture is seepage and a drainage fix is the better answer.

When a pipe is the cause, the technician checks joints, hangers, valve stems, trap connections and the water heater fittings, plus insulation that sags with moisture and drip marks on the subfloor above. Rust-streaked fittings and a sagging, soaked batt both suggest a slow drip that has been going on for a while.

Buried line leaks between the meter and the house

The buried line carries water from the meter to the house, and a leak there can run for months because nothing shows indoors. Typical signs are a meter that moves while the house is dry, a soft or unusually green strip of lawn, sunken soil and falling pressure.

Water underground does not rise straight up. Because the Kentucky Geological Survey describes the Georgetown area as karst, where water moves through dissolved limestone, the surface wet spot can be some distance from the break. Acoustic listening along the line, and tracer gas where listening is not conclusive, narrows the dig to a small area instead of a long trench.

Call Kentucky 811 before anyone digs. Repairing a leak on this line needs no permit under the state plumbing code, but a new or replacement water service does, as the service line repair page explains.

Slab leaks: a warm floor, a hiss, or a bill with no explanation

A slab leak is a leak in a pipe that runs in or under a concrete foundation slab. A leaking hot line can warm the floor above it, and a leaking cold line can leave a damp patch, lifted flooring or the sound of running water when everything is off.

Confirming one starts with isolation. The technician closes off sections of the system while watching the meter, then may use acoustic listening and thermal imaging to place the leak within a small area.

Rerouting or running a new line is new plumbing installation, which the state code requires a permit for, while repairing the leak itself does not. If the same slab has leaked before, ask whether a reroute makes more sense than another repair.

Sprinkler and irrigation line leaks

Irrigation lines run on pressure from the house supply, so a cracked head, a split buried pipe or a failing valve can raise your bill while the lawn looks fine or oddly soggy near one zone. Run the meter test with the controller off, then repeat with one zone running at a time. If the meter moves only while a zone runs, the leak is on that zone.

We locate leaks and repair the supply piping that feeds the system. Heads, controllers and zone layout are an irrigation contractor's trade.

Pool and spa leaks

Finding water loss in a pool shell, liner or the plumbing under the deck is mostly outside what this page covers, and a pool specialist is the right call. What we can do is run the meter test, check the house-side fill line and the supply to the equipment area, and tell you whether the loss is from the pool or from the plumbing that feeds it.

A wet spot near the meter box or in the yard

Water in a meter box, a soft spot at the curb or a hiss at the box may come from the meter fitting on the utility's side, or from your own pipe past it. Ask the utility to check its fitting first and tell them what you saw. Once it clears its side, the leak is on the line to your house.

Leak detection for businesses

Commercial buildings add larger meters, several risers, tenant shutoffs and sometimes limited access hours, so the work is planned differently from a house call. This page is written around homes. For shops, offices and multi-unit buildings, see commercial plumbing, and tell the dispatcher it is a business on the first call so the right technician is sent.

How the technician finds the leak without guessing

The technician may use several methods, chosen by the symptoms, because no single test fits every leak. Two methods agreeing is the standard for opening a wall or slab.

MethodWhat it findsTypical use
Pressure testWhether an isolated pipe section holds pressureConfirming a supply leak and isolating the branch.
Acoustic listeningThe sound of water escaping a pressurized pipeWalls, slabs and buried lines.
Thermal imagingTemperature differences from wet material or hot linesWarm floors, wet drywall, hot line leaks.
Tracer gasA harmless gas mixture escaping from an emptied lineLeaks too quiet to hear.
Camera inspectionCracks, breaks and roots inside a drain lineDrain leaks and damp spots with no supply cause.

A single moisture reading does not prove a leak, so results are compared before anything is cut. Camera work on drain lines is covered further under sewer inspection.
