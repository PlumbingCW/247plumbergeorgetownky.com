Drain cleaning in Georgetown, KY means removing the blockage that stops water from leaving a sink, tub, shower or floor drain, and restoring the full width of the pipe. If water is rising or standing right now, stop running anything else down the line. Our 24/7 dispatch can send a licensed plumber, and until someone arrives you can keep water use low and move belongings away from a wet floor.

A clogged drain is rarely only the plug you can see. Grease, soap film, hair and wipes stick to the pipe wall and shrink the opening a little more each week. This page covers the fixture drains inside your house, from a slow bathroom sink to a kitchen line that keeps coming back. A whole-house backup is a different job, and you can find it under main line cleaning.

Check these before you call about a clogged drain

Spend five minutes observing the house. What you notice helps the plumber guess where the blockage sits and which tool to bring.

  • Note which fixtures are slow or stopped, because one fixture points to its own branch and several point deeper.
  • Run the washing machine drain or flush a toilet to see whether another fixture gurgles or bubbles.
  • Look for water coming up in the lowest tub, shower or floor drain in the house.
  • Keep a chemical drain cleaner out of the pipe, since it can splash on a technician and sit in the trap.
  • Check whether a garbage disposal hums, and leave the reset button alone until you know why.
  • Clear the area under the sink so the trap and cleanout are easy to reach.

Emergency drain cleaning: what to do first while you wait

When a drain overflows, the first job is to stop adding water. Pause the dishwasher and washing machine, hold off on showers, and keep every sink unused. Lift rugs and electronics off the wet floor, and stay away from outlets or power strips that sit in standing water.

If sewage comes up in a tub or floor drain, treat that water as unclean. Keep children and pets out of the room and wash your hands after touching anything wet. A blockage in the main line behaves differently from a clogged fixture, so tell dispatch which drains are affected.

Kitchen sink drain clogs and where the grease goes

Kitchen lines clog differently from bathroom lines. Cooking fat leaves the sink as a warm liquid, then cools inside the pipe and coats the wall. Food scraps catch on that coating, and coffee grounds, rice and eggshells pack into the gaps until the line is nearly shut.

A double kitchen sink that drains slowly on both sides usually has its blockage past the shared trap. If only one side is slow, the problem is often in that side's tailpiece or trap arm, which a plumber can reach without cutting into a wall.

Once the line is open, a few habits help. Scrape plates into the trash, wipe pans before washing, and run cold water for a short time after the disposal stops. Hot water alone does not carry grease far enough, and it can re-melt fat that then hardens again farther down.

A kitchen line that clogs again within a few weeks needs a closer look than another quick pass with a cable. Heavy grease on the pipe wall is one reason a hydro jetting visit makes sense.

Drain snaking and cabling: how the cable reaches the blockage

A drain snake, also called a cable or auger, is a coiled steel cable that spins as it feeds into the pipe. The tip either hooks the clog and pulls it back or breaks it into pieces small enough to flow away. Sinks, tubs and floor drains use smaller cables, while longer lines call for a machine with more reach.

The technician chooses the cable head for the job. A bulb or spear head pierces a soft clog, a cutting head chews through roots, and a retrieving head pulls out hair or a fallen object. Feeling for the point where the cable meets resistance also gives a rough idea of how far down the line the blockage sits.

Cabling opens a channel through the clog but may leave a film on the pipe wall. That is fine for a one-time hair or paper clog. If the same drain stops again soon, the film is likely rebuilding and the next step is to scour the wall rather than punch another hole.

Slow drains in bathroom sinks, showers and tubs

Bathroom drains slow down gradually. Hair wraps around the stopper or pop-up rod, soap scum adds a sticky layer, and toothpaste and shaving residue pile on top. A shower that leaves ankle-deep water or a tub that takes minutes to empty is asking for attention before it stops completely.

Many bathroom clogs sit in the first foot or two, right at the stopper or in the trap, so a technician can often clear them without a long cable. A tub drain with an overflow plate can hide a clog on the other side of that plate. We check the whole fixture, not only the visible opening.

Georgetown, KY local note: cleaning out a drain does not need a permit

Kentucky plumbing rules under 815 KAR 20:050 say cleaning out waste or sewer pipes does not need a plumbing permit, while a new or replacement house sewer does. That means a clogged drain is a repair-style call you can book without paperwork.

If the camera finds a pipe that must be replaced, the job changes category and needs a permit from a licensed master plumber or an owner-occupant. When a drain line leaks instead of clogging

Not every drain problem is a blockage. A cracked or loose drain line can leak inside a wall, ceiling or crawlspace, and the signs are a stain, a musty smell, or a floor that stays damp near a fixture. Cleaning the pipe will not fix a leak, so a technician may trace the leak first, and leak detection covers that work.
