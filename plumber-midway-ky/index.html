Older houses first: what an 1840s-era town asks of its pipes

Midway was the first town in Kentucky founded by a railroad, with the land bought in 1835 and the town incorporated in 1846. The Midway Historic District, listed on the National Register in 1978, covers most of the town. That means many streets, including Railroad Street, Winter, Turner, Gratz, Dudley and Martin, hold houses and shops that may carry decades-old drain, supply and water heater work.

Older homes may have galvanized or mixed-metal supply lines, cast iron drains and patched additions that no one has mapped. A slow drain that keeps returning can point to a rough pipe wall, and a drop in pressure can point to corroded supply lines. Start with drain cleaning for stubborn blockages, and ask for a camera look if the same line clogs again.

A water heater in an older basement or crawlspace is another weak point. If the tank is rusting at the seams or the floor under it is damp, shut off the supply and call about water heater repair before a small seep becomes a flood.

Before the plumber reaches Midway: four things to do now

These steps work in a rowhouse on Main Street or a farmhouse on a back road. Do what is safe, then call the 24/7 line.

Shut off the main valve

Find the main shutoff where the line enters the house, often a basement wall or a crawlspace. Turn it clockwise until it stops, then open one low faucet to drain the pipes.

Kill power to wet areas

If water is near outlets, appliances or the panel, turn off those breakers only if you can reach them while dry. Stay out of standing water and keep children and pets away.

Clear the area and move valuables

Lift rugs, boxes and electronics off the floor and set a bucket under any drip. In an older house, check the room below for ceiling stains before moving on.

Call the 24/7 line

Have the street address, what is leaking and where the shutoff is ready. If you smell gas, leave the building first and call the gas utility emergency line or 911 from outside.

Farms and rural lots around town: ask before assuming

Horse farms such as Woodburn, Nantura and Three Chimneys surround Midway, and many rural properties may use wells, septic tanks or sump pumps instead of city service. We do not assume which one you have. When you call, tell us whether the water comes from a city line, a well or a cistern, because that decides what we bring and what we check.

A barn hydrant that freezes, a pump that cycles all night or a yard that stays soggy near a septic field each calls for different tools. We handle water lines, sump pumps and fixtures, but we do not pump septic tanks, so a full tank needs a separate hauler. For ground water in a lower level, see sump pump service.

Ask the city who serves your address

Midway city government runs its own Water, Sewer and Garbage office, and that office is the right first call about service lines and the meter. Who owns which section of pipe, from the main to your wall, is a question the city can answer for your lot. We do not guess at it.

That answer matters in an emergency because a delay in learning who owns which section of pipe is a delay in stopping a leak. Once the failed section is on your side of the meter, water service line repair is where we pick up.

Cold snaps, wet springs and the long season in between
