A new water heater is a plumbing job with a gas or electrical side, a venting side, and a permit side. Water heater installation means setting the unit, connecting water and fuel or power, adding the safety devices, and testing it against the Kentucky plumbing code.

For heat pump units, the U.S. Department of Energy asks for a room that holds between 40 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit and has roughly 1,000 cubic feet of open air. Tankless planning is covered on our tankless water heater page.

What a code-compliant install includes

The Kentucky Department of Housing, Buildings and Construction publishes a water heater packet for homeowners. These are the items it lists, and a licensed plumber should deliver each one.

  • A main shutoff valve within 3 feet of the heater.
  • A temperature and pressure relief valve within 3 inches of the top of the tank.
  • A relief discharge pipe ending within 2 inches of a floor drain, or within 4 inches of the ground.
  • An expansion tank on the cold line when a single check valve sits at the meter.
  • A corrosion-resistant drain pan when the heater is in an attic, above a drop ceiling, or on a wood floor in an occupied space.
  • A dielectric fitting where ferrous pipe meets copper.
  • A gas vent that rises 2 feet above the roof, sits 6 feet from doors and windows, and slopes upward at least a quarter inch per foot.
  • No fuel-fired heater in a bathroom or bedroom unless it is direct vent.

Gas-fired and electric installs side by side

A gas install is mostly about air and exhaust. The burner needs combustion air, the vent has to carry exhaust out of the house, and the gas connection must be leak-tested before the pilot or igniter goes live. Columbia Gas of Kentucky holds the natural gas franchise with the City of Georgetown, so service questions about the meter and the supply line go to that utility.

An electric install is mostly about the circuit. The heater needs its own breaker sized for the unit, correct wire gauge, and a proper disconnect. A panel with no spare room may need work before any heater goes in. Either way, the water side is the same: shutoff, unions, relief valve, and pan where the code calls for one.

If you smell gas near a new or old heater

Get out of the house and stay out. Standing in the yard, dial Columbia Gas of Kentucky's emergency number or 911. Do not flip switches, strike a match, or go back inside to check. Our plumbers inspect the heater once the gas utility says the building is safe.

The safety pieces people skip

These parts are small compared with the heater, and they cause large problems when left out.

Expansion tank

Heated water expands. With a check valve at the meter, that pressure has nowhere to go, so the tank absorbs it and spares the relief valve.

Relief valve discharge

The pipe has to run to a safe end point. A valve that discharges onto a finished floor or into a cap defeats its purpose.

Drain pan

In an attic or above a ceiling, a pan with a drain limits the damage when a tank eventually fails.

Dielectric fitting

It separates dissimilar metals, which slows the corrosion at the tank connections.

Planning questions to settle before install day

Start with where the heater will live. Distance from the existing hot water lines, the path for a vent, a nearby floor drain for the relief pipe, and floor material all affect the plan. A heater above living space or on a wood floor will need a pan under it, so the room for that matters too.

Next, think about how the household uses hot water. A family with several showers in the morning needs a different size from a couple in a small house. Ask us about first-hour output, not only tank size, and about recovery after heavy use.

Finally, think about what else is changing. Remodels often move kitchens and baths, and a heater is easiest to relocate while walls are open. A planned install also lets us pair the work with the permit inspection schedule instead of squeezing it in.

How an install day runs

A planned install goes in a set order. We walk through it with you on the visit.

  1. Walk-through and plan

    We confirm the location, fuel, venting path, and permit route before anything is cut.

  2. Drain and remove

    The old unit is shut off, drained, and disconnected, and the area is protected.

  3. Set and connect

    The new heater goes on its pan, with water lines, fuel or power, vent, and safety devices connected.

  4. Test and inspect

    We purge air, check for leaks, check combustion and vent draw on gas units, and set the temperature. The state inspector handles the permit inspections.

The permit, and why your installer should own it

A plumbing permit is part of every new heater job in Kentucky, because 815 KAR 20:050 lists water heaters as permit work. Homeowners may do their own, but only on the place they live, with inspections before the work is covered up. Rentals are excluded, and hiring unlicensed help can lead to prosecution under KRS 318.

When a licensed plumber sets the heater, the permit rests with the master plumber. Ask to see the master plumber's licence and the permit, and the Kentucky Division of Plumbing can confirm both. In Scott County, the state plumbing inspector keeps office hours at the Scott County Health Department on East Washington Street.

What shapes the cost of a new water heater install

Install pricing follows the house as much as the heater. These factors explain the spread between one job and the next.

Equipment choiceA basic tank, a tankless unit, and a heat pump unit sit at very different equipment costs.
Fuel and venting workNew vent runs, gas line upsizing, or a new circuit add materials and labor.
LocationAttic, crawlspace, and closet installs need more labor, and sometimes a pan and drain.
Safety partsExpansion tank, relief valve piping, and dielectric fittings are standard code items, not extras.
Permit and inspection feesThe regulation lists a $50 base fee plus $14 per additional appliance, and five inspections. Check current fees with the state.

Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden and accidental damage rather than wear, maintenance, or planned upgrades. A new heater you choose to install is generally not an insured event, so ask your insurer if you are unsure.
