A leak inside a wall is a job for leak detection first, and a pipe that has actually split calls for burst pipe repair.

Dripping tap or gushing valve: which one do you have

The two look alike at first but need very different timing. This split helps you decide how fast to move.

Plan a visit soon

  • A steady drip at the spout when the handle is off.
  • A handle that feels loose or gritty.
  • Low flow at one fixture only, often a clogged aerator.
  • Water beading around the base of a handle.

Treat it as urgent

  • A stop valve that will not close or leaks from its stem.
  • Water spraying from a supply line or connector.
  • A faucet body that has cracked or pulled loose.
  • A wet cabinet floor or ceiling below the fixture.

Burst or broken supply valve: shutting off the water right now

Under every sink and behind every toilet sits a small stop valve, often called an angle stop. Turn it clockwise to close it. These valves are made of metal and rubber, and in older homes the stem can seize or crumble when it is finally turned. If it will not close, or it keeps leaking after you close it, go to the main shutoff for the house.

The main shutoff is usually at the water meter or where the service line enters the building. Ask your utility where it is and whether you may operate the valve at the meter. GMWSS serves most of Georgetown, while Kentucky American Water serves parts of northern Scott County, so confirm which utility bills your address before an emergency happens.

After the water is off, open a faucet on the lowest floor to drain the line, mop standing water, and photograph damage for your insurer. We replace failed stop valves and supply lines at any hour, and a broken valve that cannot be closed is a reason to ask for after-hours dispatch.

Faucet repair: cartridges, washers and what hard water does to them

Most faucets leak for one of four reasons: a worn cartridge, a hardened washer or seat, a failed O-ring, or mineral buildup. Compression faucets with separate hot and cold handles use rubber washers that wear against a seat. Single-handle faucets use a cartridge that can be replaced as one unit. Which part you have depends on the brand and age, so we bring several to the visit.

GMWSS calls its water extremely hard and says it does not soften it at the plant. That water leaves a white crust on aerators, cartridges and spouts, and it can make handles stiff or cut the flow to a trickle. A clogged aerator is the easiest fix.

If scale has eaten into a cartridge, replacing the part usually costs less than replacing the whole faucet, and a softener is worth discussing if it keeps happening. Ask GMWSS or test your tap for the level at your address.

Valve and fixture repair for sinks, tubs and laundry

Fixture repair goes beyond the faucet itself. Kitchen sinks may need a new sprayer hose, a pull-down head or a cracked deck mount replaced. Bathroom vanities may need new pop-up drain assemblies, supply lines or stop valves. Utility sinks and laundry boxes have hose bibs and mixing valves that fail the same way.

When a fixture is old, we will tell you plainly whether repair is sensible or whether replacing it costs less over time. Repairing leaks, cocks and valves does not require a permit under the Kentucky plumbing regulation, but moving a fixture does. If you want a sink relocated, that is a different job and we explain the permit step before we quote it.

Where a supply line inside the wall keeps failing, a new run through whole-house repiping is sometimes the lasting fix.

Shower, tub and drain problems that start at the fixture

A shower valve that drips from the showerhead after you turn it off usually has a worn cartridge or seat inside the wall. A leak at the handle trim often points to a loose packing nut or an O-ring. Replacing a shower cartridge means shutting the water off and removing the trim, and when the valve body itself is corroded, it may need to be cut out and replaced from behind the wall.

Bathtub faucet repair follows the same logic. A tub spout that leaks when the shower is running has a diverter that no longer seals. Slow shower or tub drains are another call we get often. Hair and soap film catch in the stopper and the first few inches of pipe, and our drain cleaning team clears them without damaging the tub finish.

Before you call about a faucet or valve

A short check saves time on the phone and tells us which parts to load.

  • Note where the water comes from: spout, handle, base or the pipe below.
  • Try the stop valve under the sink and note whether it closes the flow fully.
  • Count drips for a minute. A fast drip wastes more water than it seems.
  • Check the cabinet floor for swelling, soft spots or a musty smell.
  • Write down the brand on the faucet if you can see it.
  • Take a photo of the leak and of any visible corrosion.
