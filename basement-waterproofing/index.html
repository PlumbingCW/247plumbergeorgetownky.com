A basement that smells damp, shows white crust on the walls or collects water along the floor can be dealt with, but only once you know where the water comes from. Georgetown 24/7 Plumbing offers Basement Waterproofing advice and plumbing-side work in Georgetown, KY. The goal is to move groundwater out of the structure and give it somewhere to go, not to paint over a symptom.

Basement waterproofing is a set of methods that keep groundwater from entering or collecting inside a foundation: drainage along the footing, a sump pump, sealed cracks and better grading outside. A plumber handles the drainage and pumping side. Structural problems belong to a different specialist, and we say so when we see one.

Damp walls, white crust, a musty smell or water at the floor edge

Each sign points somewhere different. Damp patches on a block or concrete wall mean moisture is moving through the masonry. White, chalky crust is efflorescence, left behind when water carries salts out of the wall and evaporates. A musty smell means damp air and possibly mold. Water along the joint where the floor meets the wall is usually groundwater pressing up from below the slab.

The technical name for the fix depends on the cause. It might be a perimeter interior drain with a sump pump, an exterior drain and membrane, injection into a single crack, or no digging at all, just gutters, downspouts and grading. The first job is to find out which, and we start by looking at where and when the water appears.

What your basement is telling you

These common signs help sort a surface problem from a pressure problem.

Wet after storms only

Likely surface water from gutters, grading or window wells. Fix outside first.

Wet for days after rain

Groundwater is rising against the foundation. A drain and sump system usually applies.

White crust on walls

Water is passing through masonry. The crust is a symptom, not the cause.

Water at the floor-wall joint

Pressure under the slab. Interior drainage handles this best.

One crack that leaks

A single crack can be injected, but check that it is not part of a larger pattern.

Musty smell, no visible water

Humidity or hidden seepage. A moisture check comes before any big work.

Limestone, rainfall and Georgetown ground

The Kentucky Geological Survey describes the Bluegrass as limestone country, where karst forms as groundwater dissolves rock and moves through fractures. Georgetown's Royal Spring is one of those karst springs. That means water can reach a foundation in pulses after a storm instead of rising evenly, which is why some basements are dry for weeks and then wet in a day.

Normal rainfall at Lexington includes about twelve days a year with an inch or more of rain, and roughly 45 inches in total. Plan for those wet days. A drain system and sump pump sized for the heaviest rains is better than one that copes only with an average week.

New subdivisions and older districts leak differently

Georgetown has historic districts such as Lane's Run and South Broadway, where older homes may sit on stone or block foundations with no drain tile at all. In those homes, seepage through the wall is a common story, and an interior drain is often the practical choice because the exterior is hard to dig around.

Newer subdivisions such as Falls Creek may have drain tile and sump pits from the start, but the soil around a newer foundation can settle for years. Backfill that sinks toward the house sends storm water down the wall. Check the grade and gutters before assuming the foundation is at fault. A good visit looks before it recommends.

When to call a structural specialist instead

A plumber does not repair foundations. Some signs mean the wall itself needs attention before any drainage work.

Call a structural or foundation specialist

  • Horizontal cracks across a block wall.
  • A wall that bows or leans inward.
  • Stair-step cracks that keep widening.
  • Doors and windows that bind after settling.

A plumber can help

  • Water entering at the floor-wall joint.
  • A sump pit that fills and a pump that fails.
  • Interior drain channel installation.
  • Discharge lines and check valves.

What to check before you call about a damp basement

Notes from you make the visit shorter and the advice more precise.

  • Note when water appears: during rain, a day after, or constantly.
  • Photograph walls, cracks and any white crust with a ruler for scale.
  • Check that downspouts discharge several feet from the house.
  • Look at the soil outside. It should slope away from the foundation.
  • Run a dehumidifier for a week and see how much water it collects.
  • Mark where you see moisture so we can compare it after storms.
