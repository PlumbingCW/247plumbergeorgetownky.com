New Construction Basement Waterproofing in Georgetown, KY

By the Georgetown 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 15, 2026 · Basement and ground

24/7 emergency line
(855) 575-2890Tap the number to call

New construction basement waterproofing in Georgetown, KY means checking and protecting a lower level that is still young, ideally before moisture shows up. Basement waterproofing is the set of drainage, sealing and water-removal measures that keep groundwater out of a below-grade space. Newer homes in subdivisions such as Falls Creek are not exempt, because the limestone ground under Scott County moves water in ways a builder's grading plan cannot fully control.

This guide is for buyers and builders. It explains what to inspect, which problems belong to a plumber and which belong to a foundation specialist, and when to involve the builder's warranty. Our basement waterproofing page covers the plumbing-side work in more detail.

Six places to look in a newer basement

A walk-through takes a few minutes and often shows the early signs of a water problem.

Floor-wall joint

A damp line, white mineral crust or a hairline gap where the slab meets the wall can show water finding a path.

Floor drain

Check that it is open, that water reaches it, and that the trap holds water.

Sump pit

If the house has one, look for a clean pit, a working float and a discharge pipe that carries water away from the foundation.

Window wells

Debris and poor drainage fill these quickly after heavy rain.

Grading outside

Soil should slope away from the walls. Settling after construction can reverse that.

Downspouts

Extensions that dump water beside the foundation load the ground with more water than it can drain.

Plumber or foundation specialist: who handles what

Honest scope matters here. A plumber is the right call for some basement water and the wrong call for others.

Plumber

  • Sump pump and discharge piping.
  • Floor drains and interior drain connections.
  • Water supply or drain leaks inside the walls.
  • Backwater and sewer-related problems.

Foundation or waterproofing specialist

  • Cracks in poured or block walls.
  • Exterior membrane and sealing.
  • Grading and exterior drainage design.
  • Structural movement or settlement.

Why a newer house can still take on water

Fresh backfill around a new foundation settles over the first seasons. As the soil sinks, it can create low spots that hold rain next to the wall. A lot graded correctly on closing day may not drain well a few years later.

Builders also work from an average soil picture, while real ground has surprises. In a limestone region the surprises include fractured rock and underground water paths that do not follow the surface slope.

If a basement feels damp in its first year, document it right away. Date photos, rainfall notes and the spot on the wall give you the record you need for any builder conversation.

Before you buy or sign off on a newer home

These steps help catch a water problem while the builder is still responsible.

  • Visit after a heavy rain if you can, and look at the basement afterward.
  • Ask whether the house has a sump pit, a drain tile system or a floor drain.
  • Check the downspouts and where they discharge.
  • Look for efflorescence, the white powder left on concrete by evaporating water.
  • Ask what the builder's warranty says about water and cracks.
  • Save the inspection report and dated photos.
  • Call Kentucky 811 before any digging or trenching, including drainage work.

When to call a licensed plumber

Call a plumber if water appears with no rain, if a sump pump runs without stopping, or if you smell sewage near a floor drain. Those point to pipes or drains, which a plumber can test. A hidden supply leak is something our leak detection work is built to find.

Call a foundation specialist for wall cracks, bowing or exterior sealing. If you are not sure which, a plumber can rule out the plumbing first and tell you honestly when the next step is not ours. Georgetown, KY local note: limestone ground and rain

The Kentucky Geological Survey explains that karst forms on limestone where groundwater dissolves the rock. Sinkholes and swallow holes carry surface water underground, and many towns were founded at karst springs. Georgetown's Royal Spring is one of them, and the town sits in the Bluegrass limestone region. Water below a Georgetown basement may travel along paths that grading plans never mapped.

The practical result is seepage, wet crawlspaces and groundwater in lower levels. Where a sump pump discharges also matters, because sewage, oil and other pollutants entering karst are not filtered. Ask the plumber where discharge water should go, and keep it away from the foundation and out of the sewer line.

Rain adds to the load. NWS climate normals for Lexington, the nearest climate station, list roughly twelve days in a typical year when an inch or more of rain falls. A basement that stays dry through light rain may still leak on those days, so test the house after a heavy rain. Before any trench or drain is dug, call Kentucky 811.
